# Degrees, Minutes, and Seconds

## A convention carried for millennia

By the late fifth century BC, Babylonian astronomers divided the Sun’s apparent path into twelve equal zodiacal signs of thirty degrees each. That makes 360 degrees for the circuit. Their arithmetic already used place value in base 60. In modern degrees, minutes, and seconds (DMS), a minute of arc is the first sixtieth of a degree, and a second of arc is the second subdivision, one sixtieth of a minute. The turn has 360 degrees, while its smaller places each use 60. For example, $1^\circ30'00''=1+\tfrac{30}{60}=\tfrac32$ degrees. [Ossendrijver, *Babylonian Astronomy: Editing and Interpreting an Ancient Science*](https://refubium.fu-berlin.de/bitstream/handle/fub188/45661/OssendrijverPages-de-609497-1-En-Online-2.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=4)

In the second century AD, Ptolemy’s *Almagest* tabulated chords from half a degree to a straight wedge in half-degree steps, using sexagesimal fractions. On a unit circle the chord subtending $2x^\circ$ has length $2\sin(x^\circ)$ for $0\le x\le90$. Bisecting that chord gives the height of the circle point reached by turning $x^\circ$ from the horizontal. The Indian sine-table tradition, exemplified by Āryabhaṭa, tabulated successive differences of radius-scaled heights. In the seventh century Bhāskara I gave a rule to calculate such a height without a table. Sexagesimal astronomical tables continued in medieval Europe, and DMS still expresses positions in astronomy and geographical coordinates. [*Almagest* I.10–11, trans. Toomer](https://terre-plate.org/library/Book%20Collection/Ptolemy_s%20Almagest%20%20%28Ptolemy%2C%20Claudius%20_%20Toomer%29.pdf) · [Shukla’s translation of Bhāskara’s rule, reproduced by Monks](https://old.maa.org/sites/default/files/images/upload_library/46/Barnett_TRIUMPHS_MiniPSPs/MiniPSP_IndianSine_2021_05_20.pdf) · [Chabás and Goldstein, *A Survey of European Astronomical Tables in the Late Middle Ages*](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~gent0113/downloads/hs2/TAC/Chabas%20%26%20Goldstein%20-%20A%20Survey%20of%20European%20Astronomical%20Tables%20in%20the%20Late%20Middle%20Ages%20%282012%29%20%5BTAC%2002%5D.pdf)

## What the degree label gives us

Murphy’s point is that the ordinary notation can hide the work of exhibiting a wedge. The label $1^\circ$ gives the exact fraction $1/360$ of a turn. The endpoint of that turn on a unit circle is a primitive $360$th root of unity, of algebraic degree $\varphi(360)=96$. Degrees of straightedge-and-compass constructions over the rationals are powers of two, so this endpoint is outside their reach. By contrast, the pentagon and equilateral triangle give $18^\circ$ and $15^\circ$ by bisection. Their difference is $3^\circ$, and one more bisection gives $1.5^\circ$.

An exact label or algebraic specification is a finite piece of information. It need not supply a finite positional expansion of the height. For example, $\sin(1^\circ)=\cos(89^\circ)$ has algebraic degree $48$. Indeed, $\zeta=\exp(2\pi i\,89/360)$ is also primitive, and adjoining it to the real field generated by $\zeta+\zeta^{-1}$ has degree two, by the quadratic with those two conjugate roots. This irrational height has no terminating decimal or sexagesimal expansion. At some other degree inputs, the height is available exactly as a rational or a [[Constructible Numbers|constructible length]]. At $30^\circ$, for instance, it is $1/2$ by the equilateral-triangle chord construction.

A table entry or computed height is warranted at a stated resolution. Writing more places can refine that answer. No finite computation exhausts the digits of an irrational height, and a task that specifies a [[Numbers, Lengths and Pixels|Pixel]] width only calls for a certified interval of that width.

## Three ways to compute a height

Use symmetry and sign to reduce to $0\le x\le90$, where $x$ is the numerical degree input. Put $p=x(180-x)$ and let $r=\pi x/180$ be the same rotation in radians.

| Rule | Formula | Uniform absolute error on the reduced interval |
|---|---|---:|
| Bhāskara I | $\dfrac{4p}{40500-p}$ | $<2\times10^{-3}$ |
| Murphy | $\dfrac{p+2.54957590808\times10^{-5}p^2+1.73688510282\times10^{-10}p^3}{10313.1717549-0.0553197291354p}$ | $<3.5\times10^{-7}$ |
| Six nonzero Taylor terms | $\displaystyle T_6(r)=\sum_{k=0}^{5}\frac{(-1)^k r^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}$ | $<5.73\times10^{-8}+\delta+\rho$ for the finite evaluation described below |

A right angle is constructible, and its radian measure is specified by $\pi/2$. Since $\pi$ is irrational, that specification cannot be exhibited as a terminating decimal. For a degree input $x$, bracket $\pi$ by rationals and multiply both endpoints by $x/180$. Use a rational $\widehat r$ from the resulting interval, with $0\le\widehat r\le11/7$ and certified distance at most $\delta$ from the radian measure. The alternating Taylor terms at $\widehat r$ decrease in magnitude. The first omitted term bounds the truncation error by $\widehat r^{13}/13!\le(11/7)^{13}/13!<5.73\times10^{-8}$. The sine bound $|\sin u-\sin v|\le|u-v|$ accounts for the conversion error $\delta$. Add the certified evaluation-rounding allowance $\rho$, which is zero when the polynomial is evaluated by exact rational arithmetic.

### [AI] Uniform certificate for the two rational rules

This check treats the displayed coefficients as exact rationals. Put $u=x/90$ so that $p=16200u-8100u^2$. Take $q=3.1415926535897932384626433832795$ and $S(u)=\sum_{k=0}^{9}(-1)^k(qu/2)^{2k+1}/(2k+1)!$. The 27- and 28-term alternating bounds in Machin’s identity $\pi=16\arctan(1/5)-4\arctan(1/239)$ give $|q-\pi|<10^{-30}$. The next sine term and that conversion error give $|S(u)-\sin(\pi u/2)|<10^{-15}$.

For each rational rule $N/D$, the denominator is positive throughout because $0\le p\le8100$. The lower bounds for $D$ are $32400$ for Bhāskara and $9865$ for Murphy. Form the degree-21 polynomial $P=N-DS$. If $P(u)=\sum_j c_j u^j$, its Bernstein coefficients on $[0,1]$ are $b_i=\sum_{j=0}^i c_j\binom{i}{j}/\binom{21}{j}$. Subdivide eight times at the midpoint. At each split, replace successive pairs by their averages until one coefficient remains. The first entries of the successive rows give the left coefficients, and the last entries in reverse order give the right coefficients. On each of the 256 subintervals, the polynomial lies between the smallest and largest coefficients because the Bernstein basis is nonnegative and sums to one.

Exact rational comparisons give maximum absolute coefficients below $32400(0.002-10^{-15})$ and $9865(0.00000035-10^{-15})$, respectively. Divide by the respective denominator lower bound and add the Taylor reference error to obtain the two bounds in the table. This certificate is established over the whole interval and can be reused for evaluations within it.

## Choose the method by the certificate and the cost

Given an allowed absolute error $E$, a rational result $v$ with a certified error at most $E$ gives the Pixel $[v-E,v+E]$ of width $2E$. At $45^\circ$, Bhāskara gives $12/17$ using rational arithmetic alone. The uniform certificate permits the interval $[12/17-1/500,12/17+1/500]$, of width $1/250$. Murphy’s rule can supply width $7\times10^{-7}$ using the displayed coefficients. A task requiring width $2\times10^{-7}$ can use six Taylor terms if the radian conversion and evaluation together are certified within $4\times10^{-8}$, with the supplied radian input bounded by $11/7$.

The two degree-based rules share the cost of forming $p$. Bhāskara then needs one multiplication, one subtraction, and one division. Murphy’s rule adds a cubic numerator and a linear denominator, both evaluable by nested multiplication. Taylor needs a radian conversion and a polynomial evaluation, which can use nested multiplication in $r^2$. Operation counts describe the arithmetic shape. The actual cost also depends on operand sizes, rounding, and whether a certificate can be reused. Count the cost of **producing** the value and the cost of **establishing or checking** its error separately. See [[Computational Admissibility]] and [[Exact Until Output]].

## Slope in terms of degrees

A wedge given by its rise and run has slope directly. A degree label gives a fraction of a turn, and the following two rules compute a rational approximation to its slope. Murphy’s **naive rule** and **exacting rule** share the form

$$R(x)=\frac{x(A+Bx^2+Cx^4)}{(8100-x^2)(1+Dx^2)},\qquad 0\le x<90.$$

| Rule | $A$ | $B$ | $C$ | $D$ | Certified relative error for $0<x<90$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Naive | $287550/2025$ | $-7/2025$ | $0$ | $0$ | $<1.3\times10^{-2}$ |
| Exacting | $141.371687709$ | $-0.00483124305851$ | $1.48893105578\times10^{-8}$ | $-1.22561463401\times10^{-5}$ | $<10^{-6}$ |

The naive rule gives $R(45)=1$, the exact slope of an isosceles right triangle. At $30^\circ$ it gives $125/216$ against the geometric slope $1/\sqrt3$. The exacting rule gives approximately $0.5773502691913$ at the same input. Both rules give zero at $x=0$, extend to negative inputs by odd symmetry, and have a pole at the right angle, where the horizontal run vanishes. In the exacting rule $1+Dx^2\ge0.90072521464519$ throughout the first quadrant.

For a relative tolerance $e<1$, a rational result $v=R(x)>0$ with $|v/\tan(x^\circ)-1|\le e$ certifies the slope interval $[v/(1+e),v/(1-e)]$. Its width is $2ve/(1-e^2)$. The width grows with the slope, so a fixed-width Pixel requires checking this width at the input or bounding it over a range separated from the right angle. With the printed coefficients even the exacting rule has an unbounded absolute error toward the pole. Its leading coefficient in $1/(90-x)$ is the rational $(A+8100B+8100^2C)/(2(1+8100D))$, while the geometric coefficient is $180/\pi$. Evaluate the naive numerator as a linear polynomial in $x^2$. The exacting rule adds one nested multiplication in that numerator and a multiplication by the extra denominator factor after forming it. Both evaluate by rational arithmetic at rational degree inputs. The interval test determines whether that extra cost buys the required width.

### [AI] Uniform certificate for the slope rules

Put $u=x/90$, $k=\pi/2$, and $\operatorname{sinc}(z)=\sin(z)/z$ with value $1$ at zero. Cancel the endpoint factors using $\cos(ku)=\sin(k(1-u))$. With $H(u)=A+8100Bu^2+8100^2Cu^4$ and $G(u)=90(1+u)(1+8100Du^2)$, the relative error is

$$\frac{R(x)}{\tan(x^\circ)}-1
=\frac{H(u)\operatorname{sinc}(k(1-u))-G(u)\operatorname{sinc}(ku)}{G(u)\operatorname{sinc}(ku)}.$$

The denominator exceeds $50$ on $[0,1]$. Indeed $1+8100Du^2\ge0.9$ and $\operatorname{sinc}(ku)\ge 2/\pi$ by concavity of sine on the first quadrant. For the numerator use the rational polynomial $V(u)=\sum_{j=0}^{9}(-1)^j(qu/2)^{2j}/(2j+1)!$, with the same rational $q$ as in the height certificate. The alternating remainder and $|q-\pi|<10^{-30}$ give a uniform error less than $10^{-15}$ for $V(u)$ against $\operatorname{sinc}(ku)$. For the conversion term one can use $\operatorname{sinc}(z)=\int_0^1\cos(tz)\,dt$, which gives a Lipschitz bound of $1/2$.

Apply the same eight midpoint subdivisions used for the height certificate to $H(u)V(1-u)-G(u)V(u)$, using its actual degree in the Bernstein coefficient formula. Exact rational comparisons bound the largest absolute coefficient by $0.642$ for the naive rule and $0.00004555$ for the exacting rule. Since $|H|+|G|<400$, add $400\times10^{-15}$ for the two reference errors and divide by $50$. These bounds yield the relative tolerances in the table over the whole open quadrant.
