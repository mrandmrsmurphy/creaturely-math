# Corrective Narrative

*What follows is a corrective account — corrective against both the secular rationalist narrative (Greeks invent reason; Dark Ages suppress it; Enlightenment rescues it) and against a naïve Christian triumphalism that pretends each step was a neat improvement over the last. Murphy applies the same "Harrison lessons" to mathematics that Peter Harrison applies to the history of science: the standard periodization is anachronistic, the conflict thesis is fictional, and the medieval and Islamic periods are systematically underread. See peter-harrison.*

### The Babylonians: Praxis-Rich, Apodeixis-Light — Not Inferior, Earlier

The standard account places the Babylonians in a kind of mathematical prehistory — people who could calculate but didn't yet understand what they were doing. This is wrong, and condescending in a way Murphy's Quadriga makes visible.

The Babylonians had robust Quaestio (they knew what kind of question they were asking), sophisticated Noesis (they spotted structural patterns across problems), and exceptional Praxis (they executed). What they were **very light on was Apodeixis** — the art of formal demonstration. They did not write proofs. What they wrote was procedure: here is the method; here is the result; here is the verification that it fits. But the absence of proof is not the absence of mathematics.

The evidence is substantial:

- **Plimpton 322** (c. 1800 BC) — a clay tablet listing Pythagorean triples (integer solutions to $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$), generated systematically, centuries before Pythagoras. One reading, Mansfield's, which Murphy subscribes to, is that it is a rational trigonometry table — exact ratios rather than angle approximations, aligning with exactly the kind of certificate-explicit thinking Murphy's framework values over the irrational-approximation approach that later displaced it. Other historians of Babylonian mathematics read the tablet differently.
- **YBC 7289** — a tablet computing $\sqrt{2}$ as 1.41421296..., accurate to six decimal places, using successive rational approximations with explicit error-bounding — precisely Murphy's "rational approximations with explicit error bounds" from the position statement above.
- **Compound interest tables** — the Babylonians solved exponential growth problems, anticipating what modern mathematics calls logarithmic thinking.
- **Frustum and cylinder volumes** — computing volumes of truncated cones and cylinders requires integration-like reasoning: the accumulation of infinitely thin slices. The Babylonians did this with explicit algorithms, producing correct results without a formal theory of limits. Praxis that anticipates Noesis by two millennia.

The theological framing: the Babylonians were building in the Terrestrial Studies band — mathematics as a tool for land, commerce, astronomy, and construction. Their mathematics was embedded in the creation-mandate work of a civilization. They were not waiting for the Greeks to teach them what mathematics really was. They were doing the same four arts — weighted heavily toward Praxis, lightly on Apodeixis — and their light Apodeixis is not a failure but a stage. The cathedral takes generations.

### The Greeks: Apodeixis as Doxology — Not as Mastery

The Greeks did not invent mathematics. They invented the *proof* — Apodeixis as a formal art. This is a genuine and enormous contribution, and it deserves the honor it receives. But the standard reading misses the theological register in which it was made.

When the proof was new — when Thales or his successors first demonstrated a geometric truth by inexorable logical necessity — the response was not hubris. It was worship. The tradition is that a discovery in right-angled triangles was met with a **sacrifice of oxen**. Diogenes Laertius (*Lives of Eminent Philosophers* 1.24–25) reports: "Pamphila states that, having learnt geometry from the Egyptians, he [Thales] was the first to inscribe a right-angled triangle in a circle, whereupon he sacrificed an ox." He adds that others, among them Apollodorus the arithmetician, tell this of Pythagoras and his theorem, and elsewhere (8.12) the sacrifice for Pythagoras is "a famous sacrifice of oxen", which later writers retell as a hecatomb. So it may not have been a hecatomb, and it may be one ox, for Thales. The details are almost certainly unreliable, but the story is theologically accurate in its meaning. The Pythagoreans, and the Greek mathematical tradition they founded, received the discovery of proof *as a revelation*: the world had a rational structure, and the human mind could see it. That was cause for awe, not for ownership.

This is the sapiential posture of [[Five Acts]] still. The Greeks who built Euclidean geometry were, in their best moments, sapiential mathematicians: they thought they were reading a book, not writing one. Plato's philosophy, for all its dangers, located mathematical objects in a realm of divine mind — which is not the same as claiming human minds had mastered them. The Forms were above, not below.

Apodeixis in Greek mathematics is doxological before it is anything else. It is the moment at which the creature can say: *I have found something that must be true, and I did not make it so.* Q.E.D. is a little eschatology in any age.

**What the Greeks did not have:** an explicit theory of what kind of objects they were working with. The tension between the discrete (number, $\mathbb{N}$, $\mathbb{Z}$) and the continuous (geometric magnitude, the constructible real lengths) was never fully resolved in antiquity — it was largely suppressed. Euclid keeps arithmetic and geometry in separate books precisely because the foundations are uneasy. The Pythagorean discovery that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational (not a ratio of integers) is said to have prompted a crisis severe enough that the offending student was drowned at sea. Whether or not the drowning is historical, the crisis is: the Greeks found the certificate problem without the vocabulary to name it.

### Christians: Not a Step Back

The "Dark Ages" thesis — that Christianity suppressed Greek learning and that medieval Europe was a mathematical waste — is a 19th-century polemical construction, exactly Harrison's argument applied to mathematics. The actual record:

- The **quadrivium** — arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy — was formalized as part of Christian education by Boethius (c. 524 CE) and remained the curriculum of cathedral schools and monasteries throughout the medieval period. Mathematics was not abandoned; it was institutionalized as Christian formation.
- **Bede** (c. 672–735) computed the ecclesiastical calendar — a non-trivial mathematical task requiring astronomical precision — and wrote extensively on computation.
- **The University** is a Christian invention. Bologna (1088), Paris (c. 1150), Oxford (c. 1167) — all Christian institutions. The university system preserved and transmitted mathematics as a matter of theological commitment: because God made an ordered world, learning the order of that world was an act of devotion. Hugh of Saint Victor's *Didascalicon* (c. 1125) is the explicit statement of this thesis: see raw/Drafts/didascalicon.md.
- **Cathedral geometry** — the engineering mathematics required to build Gothic arches, flying buttresses, and ribbed vaulting was sophisticated. The cathedral builders were doing real applied mathematics without a formal framework, in exactly the Babylonian mode: Praxis-rich, Apodeixis-light, excellent Noesis.

The Christian period did not advance the formal theory of proof beyond Euclid. That is true. But the period preserved, transmitted, institutionalized, and embedded mathematics in a formation culture that kept the doxological register alive. That is not nothing.

### Islam: Preservation and Invention

The Islamic Golden Age (roughly 8th–13th centuries) is typically credited with "preserving Greek learning" — a formulation that damns with faint praise. Islamic mathematicians did preserve Greek learning, and they transmitted it to Europe via translation. But that is not all they did.

- **Al-Khwarizmi** (c. 780–850) — his treatise *Al-Kitāb al-mukhtaṣar fī ḥisāb al-jabr wal-muqābala* gives us the words *algebra* (al-jabr) and *algorithm* (from his Latinized name, Algoritmi). He developed systematic methods for solving linear and quadratic equations — a genuine extension of Greek and Babylonian work, not a transcription of it.
- **Al-Biruni** (c. 973–1048) — his trigonometry used rational methods and explicit error-bounding with a sophistication that parallels what Mansfield finds in Plimpton 322. His insistence on measurement, verification, and explicit procedure is, in Murphy's framework, strong Praxis discipline.
- **Omar Khayyam** (c. 1048–1131) — solved cubic equations geometrically, using conic sections. This places him directly before Cardano in the cubic story, providing the geometric certificate that Cardano's algebraic method would later abandon.
- **Ibn al-Haytham (Alhazen)** (c. 965–1040) — his *Book of Optics* introduced the experimental method into natural philosophy centuries before the European scientific revolution.

The Islamic mathematical tradition was doing sapiential mathematics in the fullest sense: receiving the created order, extending the tools of understanding, with no particular aspiration to replace the Creator as the source of mathematical truth.

### Cardano and the Fracture: The First Bamboozlement

Girolamo Cardano's *Ars Magna* (1545) is the first major moment at which a mathematical object was admitted into calculation **without a construction certificate** — purely because it worked.

The problem: the general cubic equation ($x^3 + px + q = 0$) can be solved by a formula. But the formula, even when the final answer is a real (constructible) number, sometimes passes through intermediate steps involving the square root of a negative number. Cardano could not construct $\sqrt{-1}$. No one could. It has no geometric interpretation (the Wessel, Argand, and Gauss Complex Plane came two and a half centuries later). Cardano called these numbers *numeri sophistici* — "sophistic numbers," fake, useless, and yet undeniably productive.

He used them anyway.

Rafael Bombelli (1572) systematized their arithmetic, developing rules for operating with $\sqrt{-1}$ as if it were a real object — while acknowledging that it was not, in any construction-certificate sense, real.

This is the fracture in the certificate-explicit tradition. Not yet repression — Cardano and Bombelli were visibly troubled by what they were doing. But it is the first instance of the logic that the posture of repression systematizes: *it works; therefore use it; certificate questions are someone else's problem.* The "sophistic numbers" represent the moment when expressive power was acquired by cheating on the certificate — and the cheat was both successful and disturbing to those who made it.

The eventual resolution — the Wessel, Argand, and Gauss Complex Plane, giving imaginary numbers a genuine two-dimensional geometric construction — restores certificate-explicitness. A complex number $a + bi$ is a point in the plane; the plane is constructible; equality is geometric. The bamboozlement was temporary and eventually honest. But the three centuries between Cardano and the Complex Plane were conducted on borrowed credentials, which is exactly the dynamic the posture of repression ([[Five Acts]]) extends into a permanent policy.

*Note for teaching:* This case is pedagogically rich because it shows the certificate problem is not merely theoretical — it has a concrete, graspable historical instance, and the eventual resolution (the Complex Plane) demonstrates that the creaturely-realist approach can recover legitimate objects from apparent bamboozlements, rather than simply refusing them.
