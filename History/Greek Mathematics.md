# Greek Mathematics

The third stage of [[Historical Order]], after [[Tally Marks]] and [[Babylonian Mathematics]]. **Dates: about 600 BCE to 500 CE**, from Thales to Boethius; the next stage is [[Mathematics 500 to 1500]]. All historical attributions and dates here are from recollection and **to be checked**.

## Dates (about 600 BCE to 500 CE; all approximate)

| Period | People and works |
|---|---|
| **c. 600–400 BCE** | Thales (c. 624–546); Pythagoras (c. 570–495) and the brotherhood; Parmenides and Zeno (c. 490–430); Hippocrates of Chios, lunes (c. 430); Philolaus |
| **c. 400–300 BCE** | Plato (c. 428–348; Academy c. 387); Theaetetus; **Eudoxus** (c. 408–355; proportion, exhaustion); Aristotle (384–322; potential infinity); **Euclid** (c. 300; *Elements*) |
| **c. 300–150 BCE** | **Archimedes** (c. 287–212; *Method*, *Sand Reckoner*); Eratosthenes (c. 276–194; sieve, the earth's size); **Apollonius** (c. 262–190; *Conics*); Hipparchus (c. 190–120; trigonometry, Babylonian records) |
| **c. 50 BCE–200 CE** | Heron of Alexandria (c. 10–70 CE; practical geometry, root method); Nicomachus (c. 100 CE; *Introduction to Arithmetic*); Theon of Smyrna; Ptolemy (c. 100–170; *Almagest*) |
| **c. 200–500 CE** | Diophantus (dated c. 200–290; *Arithmetica*); Pappus (c. 320; *Collection*); Theon of Alexandria and Hypatia (c. 355–415); Proclus (412–485; commentary on Euclid I); Eutocius (c. 500; commentaries on Archimedes) |

Boethius (c. 480–524) stands at the boundary and begins the next stage. The Hellenistic centre was Alexandria from about 300 BCE, not Athens, and the Greek mathematical world after about 150 BCE overlapped Babylonian astronomy and the Roman state.

## The spine

> **Greek mathematics is a great human achievement that carried a metaphysics which the vault separates from the method.** Keep the method: proof, construction, exact surds, and the arithmetic of whole numbers. Refuse the claim attached to it: that reasoning well gives the knower God's-eye view, or that what is defined exists.

Three things are meant to be said together: (1) truth is eternal but our access to it is historied; (2) the posture is reception and investigation, not existence by fiat; (3) the Greeks were neither secular nor a sharp break from the Babylonians, and they had **two** mathematical traditions, not one.

## 1. Truth is eternal; our access to it is historied

*Truth is truth and it is eternal.* What changes is **we**. Murphy's two illustrations:

- **Notation.** $2+2=4$ is a later form of $II+II=IIII$ ([[Tally Marks]]). The truth did not change; the way creatures hold it did.
- **Revelation.** The Trinity is taken as a **compatible deepening** of what the Shema (Dt 6:4) asserts of a strict unity, and not as a change in God. A caution [AI]: the analogy to notation has a limit. The Trinity is not a re-notation of the same fact; it is the disclosure of **who** God is, and the vault's own position on one and many is that unity and plurality are *equally ultimate* in God ([[Theology of Number]]). So the parallel is "same truth, fuller access", not "same fact, new symbols."

**The history of proof shows the access changing.** Euclid's proofs were thought complete for about two thousand years, and they contained hidden assumptions (about betweenness and continuity) that were made explicit only in the 19th century (Pasch, 1882; Hilbert, 1899; recollection). That is the story Kline tells in *Loss of Certainty*. A proof is a **dated human act**; what it points to is not. So the claim "a well-reasoned proof is ahistorical, universal and eternal" is *close to true*: the truth proved is eternal; the proof, as ours, is finite, correctable and historical ([[Quadriga for Mathematics]]: "I am not trustworthy alone").

**The wrong form.** The error is to read the eternity of the truth as an eternity of the *knower's* grasp, so that doing logic well makes one like God. In Greek thought this is the ascent by contemplation (the Pythagorean and Platonic path, and in the later Neoplatonists a union with the One). It is the Promethean act of [[Five Acts]]. Kepler's "thinking God's thoughts after him" is the same activity *received*, and the difference is the posture.

### What and who (a side claim) [AI]

Murphy ties the wrong form to a theological point: that confessional definitions of God (the Westminster Confession, chapter 2) answer **what** God is where Scripture answers **who** he is (the name in Exodus 3, narrative, command, promise). I would keep this as a side claim for three reasons: chapter 2 is built from scriptural proof texts and chapter 2.3 confesses three persons; Shorter Catechism Q4 literally asks "What is God?"; and many historians of Reformed theology resist reading classical theism as Hellenization. A form of the point that does not depend on settling that: **mathematics defined from first principles (what an object is) differs from mathematics told as a history of construction (how we came to it)**, and the vault is on the side of the history. The confession is also cited elsewhere in the vault for God's infinity ([[Theological Grounds]]); the two uses agree if the *content* is taken as biblical and only the *method of defining* is the risk.

## 2. Existence by fiat

Modern axiomatic mathematics lets existence be **declared**: Hilbert's stance, as I recall from the Frege correspondence, was that if the axioms are consistent the things they define exist. Murphy's charge: this is **a Christian heresy**, because to bring a thing into being by declaring it is the Creator's act (*fiat lux*, Gen 1:3). The posture the vault wants is **reception and investigation**.

**Greek geometry is on the vault's side of this.** Euclid's first postulates are *constructions*: to draw a line between two points, to extend it, to draw a circle. And Proposition I.1 does not assert that an equilateral triangle exists; it **constructs** one from two circles. The existence criterion of the *Elements* is constructibility, which is exactly the constructible field $\mathbb K$ ([[Constructible Numbers]]). **The Greeks asked for acts; modern axiomatics asks for consistency.**

Two qualifications [AI]:

- **The vault allows fiat numbers when labelled.** [[Fiat Numbers]] adjoins $\varepsilon$, $i$, $j$ *by declaration*, with the declaration named and, where possible, a model exhibited ($i$ as a quarter turn). So the target of the charge is *unlabelled* fiat presented as discovery, and existence claimed where no model is exhibited, not every declared extension.
- **"Heresy" is a theological verdict**, and a strong one. On the page it is recorded as Murphy's term. What can be shown mathematically is the contrast above (construction versus consistency), and the verdict follows only given the theology.

### Praxis, and mathematics as a story

*Praxis*, the doing, is a glory and not a lesser part ([[Quadriga for Mathematics]]): Jesus is called *ho tektōn*, the carpenter (Mk 6:3; the *tekton* is a builder, to check). Mathematics is a **practice with a history**, and the discipline "seems unwilling to recognize that math is a historied story"; the vault embraces it. That is why the history stages of [[Historical Order]] are part of the curriculum, and why a construction in the Greek sense is a Praxis.

## 3. The Greeks were not secular scientists

The tale is *the Greeks were secular scientists, and the Christian dark ages stopped them, so that we would be living on the moon by now.* It descends from the nineteenth-century "conflict thesis" of Draper and White and from Huxley's retelling. **Peter Harrison** has documented its failures: *The Territories of Science and Religion* (Chicago, 2015) argues that "science" and "religion" are modern categories projected backward, and *The Bible, Protestantism, and the Rise of Natural Science* (Cambridge, 1998) argues that the biblical and Protestant reading of nature helped the rise of natural science (both from recollection; to be checked and added to [[Reading List]]). Related: Lindberg, *The Beginnings of Western Science*; Numbers, *Galileo Goes to Jail*; Russell, *Inventing the Flat Earth* (the flat-earth myth).

On the Greeks specifically:

- **They were religious.** Pythagoreanism was a brotherhood with a cult and a way of life; Plato's *Timaeus* has a divine craftsman; and the legend of the sacrifice of an ox for a geometric discovery is a *doxological* story ([[Theology of Number]]). Proclus, whose commentary on Euclid is a main source for the early history, was a Neoplatonist who wrote theology.
- **Number was a theology.** Nicomachus's *Introduction to Arithmetic* and its tradition treat numbers as divine and symbolic.
- **Christians did not lose it.** The quadrivium was taught, texts were copied, translated and extended, and universities were founded (to check).

## 4. The Greeks and the Babylonians: a smaller break

Murphy's claim, against even Kline's emphasis: there was **not as big a break** as usually said, *if one looks outside the early books of Euclid.* Evidence, from recollection:

| Greek | Babylonian continuity |
|---|---|
| **Heron**'s square-root method | the Babylonian method of averaging (the algorithm of [[Babylonian Mathematics]]) |
| **Ptolemy**'s *Almagest* and chord tables | sexagesimal fractions throughout ([[Sexagesimal]]) |
| **Hipparchus** | used Babylonian eclipse and period records |
| **Diophantus**, *Arithmetica* | problem-solving with rational numbers in the style of the Babylonian tablets |
| **The Pythagorean theorem** | known and *used* on the Babylonian tablets ([[Babylonian Mathematics]], Plimpton 322); the Greek novelty is the **proof**, and the demand for one |

So what is new is not the content but the **requirement of demonstration**, and its home in a philosophy of the One. [AI] A caution: historians still dispute how to weigh the continuity (the "Greek miracle" against Neugebauer-style emphasis on the Babylonian algebra), and whether Euclid Book II is *geometric algebra* (a disguised algebra) or simply geometry (the objection associated with Unguru, 1975; recollection). The vault says Book II **can be read as** the first algebra, and does not say it *is* algebra.

## 5. Two traditions, and the need for both

| | **The geometry and proof tradition** | **The number and computation tradition** |
|---|---|---|
| **Texts** | Euclid I–VI and XI–XIII, Apollonius, Eudoxus's theory of proportion | Nicomachus, Theon of Smyrna, Diophantus, Heron, Ptolemy, Eratosthenes, Archimedes (*Method*, *The Sand Reckoner*) |
| **Objects** | magnitudes (lengths, areas) | whole numbers and ratios |
| **Method** | deduction from postulates and constructions | procedures, tables, approximations with errors |
| **Home in the vault** | [[Constructible Trigonometry]], exact surds | [[Discrete Calculus]], [[Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic]], [[Modular Arithmetic]] |

**Euclid himself has both.** Books VII–IX are *arithmetic* (the Euclidean algorithm, the infinitude of primes, the pieces of unique factorization), written with numbers as line segments. Book V's theory of proportion (Eudoxus) is the bridge. Book X classifies incommensurables. The *Elements* has been among the most widely printed books in history (the claim that it was second only to the Bible is commonly repeated and I cannot confirm it), and that fame has made the geometry look like the whole of Greek mathematics. **We need both.**

### Worked examples (all checked)

**Side and diagonal numbers** (the number tradition's $\sqrt2$; Theon, Plato's *Republic*; recollection). Start with $s=d=1$ and repeat $s'=s+d$, $d'=2s+d$:

| $s$ | 1 | 2 | 5 | 12 | 29 | 70 | 169 | 408 | 985 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $d$ | 1 | 3 | 7 | 17 | 41 | 99 | 239 | 577 | 1393 |
| $d^2-2s^2$ | $-1$ | $1$ | $-1$ | $1$ | $-1$ | $1$ | $-1$ | $1$ | $-1$ |

Each $d/s$ approximates $\sqrt2$ and the error is **certified by an exact integer identity**: $d^2-2s^2=\pm1$ gives $\left|\tfrac ds-\sqrt2\right|<\tfrac1{2s^2}$ (for $\tfrac{1393}{985}$ the error is $3.6\times10^{-7}$, the bound $5.2\times10^{-7}$). This is an *approximation with a stated error*, the Babylonian stance with a Greek proof. The Babylonian $1;24,51,10$ is a different approximation of the same number.

**Euclid's algorithm applied to $\sqrt2$.** The continued fraction of $\sqrt2$ is $[1;2,2,2,\dots]$, **never ending** (checked for eight terms), and its convergents $1,\tfrac32,\tfrac75,\tfrac{17}{12},\tfrac{41}{29},\tfrac{99}{70}$ are exactly the side and diagonal numbers. For a ratio of whole numbers the same subtraction process *stops* (e.g. $\gcd(252,198)=18$, Book VII); for the diagonal it **never stops**. This is, I recall, the Greek meaning of "incommensurable": the algorithm of Book X.2 does not terminate.

**Eudoxus's test for equal ratios** (Book V, Definition 5, recollection) says $a:b=c:d$ when for **all** whole numbers $m,n$, $ma>nb$ exactly when $mc>nd$. Comparing $\sqrt2:1$ with $99:70$: take $m=70$, $n=99$. Then $70\sqrt2<99$ (since $2\cdot70^2=9800<9801=99^2$) but $70\cdot99=99\cdot70$, so the test fails, and the two ratios are **not equal**: a certificate of inequality *given by a pair of whole numbers and checked in integers*. Note the shape: **inequality is certified by a finite check, equality never is** (it would need all $m,n$) unless both ratios are rational, when cross-multiplication settles it. This is Group B of the [[Number Hierarchy]] ("inequality certificates; equality is asymptotic") stated by Eudoxus.

**Archimedes' *The Sand Reckoner*** opens (as I recall) by addressing those who think the number of grains of sand is *infinite*, and answers that it is not only numerable but computable. He builds a notation from the myriad $10^4$ through the myriad-myriad $10^8$, whose full range reaches $10^{8\times10^{16}}$, and estimates the grains filling the sphere of the fixed stars at about $10^{63}$ (in his cosmology). **This is the argument of Genesis 15:5, innumerable is not infinite, in a Greek text**, and a notation reaching far past any number one can hold. It is the Greek form of [[Creational Ultrafinitism]]'s point that notation and number are different.

## What the vault keeps and refuses

| Keeps | Refuses |
|---|---|
| Proof as a repeatable, correctable act (Apodeixis) | Proof as ascent: reasoning well as becoming like God |
| Existence by **construction** (Euclid I.1) | Existence by **declaration** without a model |
| Exact surds, the constructible field | The reals as one object that hides the sort ([[Numbers, Lengths and Pixels]]) |
| Euclid VII's algorithm; the arithmetic books | "All is number" as a metaphysics |
| Eudoxus's certificate of inequality | A completed continuum read off from it |
| The number tradition (Heron, Diophantus, Archimedes) beside Euclid | Euclid alone as the whole of Greek mathematics |

## The One and the many

The Greek philosophical stance **let the One triumph over the many**: Parmenides' being, the unchanging Forms, "all is number" (the Pythagoreans). The Pythagorean programme broke on the diagonal (the tradition of Hippasus is a legend and to be checked), and Zeno's paradoxes press the same question about the continuum ([[Numbers, Lengths and Pixels]]: the discrete-versus-continuous heart of the philosophy). The Christian answer of [[Theology of Number]] is that the **One and the Three are equally ultimate**, so that neither the One nor the many may be allowed to triumph; mathematics begins in epistemological humility. This is the statement the table in that page makes.

## Teaching [AI]

1. **Euclid in the freshman year** ([[Curriculum Plan]]), read with a notebook of constructions: each proposition *done* with a compass and straightedge before it is read as proof (Praxis before Apodeixis).
2. **The number books beside it**: the Euclidean algorithm, the infinitude of primes (VII–IX).
3. **Side and diagonal numbers** as an arithmetic project: generate them, check $d^2-2s^2=\pm1$, compare with $1;24,51,10$.
4. ***The Sand Reckoner*** as a project: build the notation, find the number of grains in a cup, in a lake, in a sphere of a given radius.
5. **Say what Euclid assumed**: have students find a gap (betweenness) in a proof, and meet the history of rigor.

## Hard problems

1. **How much of the break is real.** The continuity with Babylon is Murphy's reading and has support (above), but the weight is contested.
2. **The "heresy" language.** Whether the vault states it as a *claim* or as a *position*, and how it relates to [[Fiat Numbers]].
3. **The Greek sources** (Proclus, Nicomachus, Theon, Diophantus, Heron, Archimedes) have to be read in translation; attributions here are recollections.
4. **Ancient testimony about early Pythagoreanism is late and layered.** Claims about Pythagoras and Hippasus are traditions.
