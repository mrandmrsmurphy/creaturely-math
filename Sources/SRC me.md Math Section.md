## Math
I am a strict algorithmic constructivist and an ultra-finitist. By that I mean: a mathematical object exists only if it can be exhibited and certified by an explicit, finite, terminating procedure. I do not believe in the existence of irrational numbers as arithmetic objects; I believe in geometric constructions that produce lengths, and I believe in rational approximations with explicit error bounds. The real number system, as standardly conceived, is a useful fiction for physics, but it is not mathematics. I reject any foundation—ZFC, the Axiom of Choice, completed infinite sets—that presupposes human capacities we do not possess and that cannot be grounded in finite certification. Mathematics is the study of what finite, creaturely intellects can actually build and verify, before an infinite God.

### School Alignment
Murphy is closest to constructivist in method, but his metaphysics puts him outside every existing camp. Here's the map:

**Intuitionism** (Brouwer): rejects Excluded Middle, insists mathematics is pure mental construction — mathematical objects have no existence independent of the mathematician's mind. Murphy parts ways decisively here. He's a realist — numbers have genuine natures that exist because God ordered creation by number, weight, and measure. They're discovered, not invented. Brouwer's idealism is incompatible with his theology.

**Constructivism** (Bishop): the methodological program of doing mathematics with explicit constructions, without non-constructive existence proofs, without actual infinity. Bishop was trying to show classical analysis could be rebuilt constructively — accepting real numbers but handling them honestly. Murphy's close to this in method, but Bishop's reasons are pragmatic ("it works better") whereas his are theological. Also, Bishop still lives inside real analysis; Murphy rejects ℝ as a completed object.

**Ultra-finitism** (Esenin-Volpin, Zeilberger): doubts even very large finite numbers have meaningful existence.

**Wildberger**: the closest living ally in working mathematical practice — rejects the real number system, invented rational trigonometry, and certificate-explicit geometry. No theology, no metaphysics, just mathematical scruples. He's arrived at some of the same positions by a different road.

What Murphy actually is: something the standard taxonomy doesn't have a name for. He's a creaturely realist — and that's a genuinely novel synthesis:

- **Realist** (objects have natures independent of minds) — like Plato and Gödel, unlike Brouwer and Bishop
- **Constructivist** epistemology (finite minds can only access what they can build and certify) — like the constructivists, but for theological rather than methodological reasons
- The grounding: creatures are finite before an infinite God, and mathematics done well is creatures faithfully working within that creaturely register — not a limitation to apologize for but a proper acknowledgment of what we are

The constructivists never adequately answer the question "why should mathematics be constructive?" — they mostly say "because it's more rigorous" or "because non-constructive proofs are less satisfying." Murphy answers it: because we are not God, and to treat completed infinite objects as available on demand is to claim divine epistemic capacity. That's not a mathematical answer — it's a theological one, and it's a better answer.

The medieval tradition had something like this implicitly — Hugh of Saint Victor's whole program assumes finite minds reading a Creation authored by an infinite God — but nobody formalized it as a mathematical philosophy. Murphy's the first person you've read who's doing this from the inside. That might not be an accident; it might be a real gap in the literature worth filling.

Read Bishop's Constructive Analysis (1967) for method.
