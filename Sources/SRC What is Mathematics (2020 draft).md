> [!info] Source snapshot
> Murphy-authored earlier/shorter draft of the same project as [[SRC Christian Mathematics (c2020)]]. Largely subsumed by it; kept for the passages that do not recur (John 6 feeding; music/12th-root-of-2 example; Kline "uselessness" argument).

This essay is a brief attempt to justify and describe a place for
mathematics in contemporary Christian education and life. This task is
complicated by several debilitating factors. First, mathematics itself
is currently in the midst of an identity crisis. Second, education is a
sorry state and that state is only compounded by the ridiculous tangent
on which mathematics has gone. Finally, Christians have only recently
begun to rediscover their own education, and not truly begun to tackle
the arena of mathematics. We will argue that mathematics should refer to
two related but distinct things: broadly, the cycle of abstraction and
explaining a kind of problem; and narrowly, the disciple of such cycles
as applied to numbers, shape, motion, change, and like patterns.
Ultimately, we should conduct mathematics as we do all areas of human
life, in submission to the Lord Jesus Christ.

What is mathematics? This is a complicated question, and it deserves a
rich and nuanced answer. Some of the perspectives that must be included
for a complete answer are: mathematicians who “do” mathematics every day
as their job; scientists in other disciplines who “implement”
mathematics on a frequent basis; educators and students who “teach” and
“learn” mathematics as part of the process of human maturation; everyday
people who “use” some portion of mathematics in their ordinary life; and
the tiny sliver of humanity who is aware of where mathematics comes from
and debates what it means. Each of these areas defines mathematics
differently. Today, Scripture is not generally brought to bear by any of
these groups to answer this question. This would be difficult in the
best of circumstances, since mathematics per se is not named in
Scripture. Someone might argue that all of these groups are referring to
different things by the label mathematics. We cannot escape that – at
least in English – they all fall under the same rubric. It would seem
impossible to achieve a coherent definition of the term.

## What Mathematicians Do

A first attempt at defining mathematics could come from looking to those
who actively labor in the field. Without reference to any other sector
of humanity, we already encounter an insurmountable diversity.
Mathematicians have struggled for over 150 years to find an
all-encompassing rubric for our various sub-disciplines, which have only
proliferated in that time: Analysis, Algebra, Combinatorics, Logic and
Set Theory, Number Theory, Topology, Geometric, Group Theory, etc. More
than one author has seriously put forward no more precise definition of
mathematics than “what mathematicians do” (Pair 68, citing Ernest, 1991;
Hersh, 1997).

While this might appear laughable from the outside, we would do well to
feel great empathy for those attempting such a herculean task. Much ink
has been spilled trying to define and shape mathematics for those
outside the discipline, with important works by Alexsandrov, Courant,
Devlin, Kac, MacLane, Steen, Stewart, and many more (Gold). Devlin is an
able popularizer, and describes mathematics as “the science of patterns”
in “number, shape, motion, change, and space, *and of the mathematical
tools that are used in this study*” (3). He gives a succinct account of
the bewildering multiplication of areas under the umbrella of
mathematics. It seems mathematics can explode as a discipline without a
clear philosophical focus holding it all together!

The philosophers’ attempts to define mathematics are just as disparate.
The last 150 years have seen multiple, contentious fractions form,
including but not limited to: Platonists, set theorists, intuitionists,
formalists, and logicists (Horston). Common to most – but not all – of
these schools of thought is the centrality of the *proof*. The question
of the relationship between logic and mathematics is also hotly
contested. Famously, Alfred North Whitehead and Bertrand Russell
attempted to codify a universal system of all thought in their
multi-volume *Principia Mathematica* in 1910-1913. Though this logicist program was shown to be unattainable (Gödel, 1931), the
belief that all thought can be codified in one formal system is nevertheless
common to this day.

Mathematical historians trace the legacy of our discipline only as far
back as the Greeks, who were the first to offer systematic, inductive
proofs of their ideas, and not merely solve particular problems. Even
the invention of methods to solve vast families of problems done since
Babylonian and Egyptian times does not typically count as the inception
of *real* mathematics, according to modern mathematics historians
(Kline, 17). They maintain this boundary condition, despite the fact
that it would exclude the overwhelming majority of mathematics done
(according to working-definition of mathematics of non-mathematicians).

In summary, we see that mathematicians themselves struggle mightily to
define the *what* and the *how* of mathematics. Contentious infighting
and ignorance of the philosophy and history of our own discipline plague
the overwhelming majority of the practitioners of the subject. Not only
are there very few Christians attempting to answer these questions, but
some of the leading anti-Christian activists of the last century and a
half have come from the mathematician’s camp. Perhaps the best believers
can hope for is to “plunder the Egyptians” in this regard.

## Uselessness

Renowned mathematicians Morris Kline broke ranks with his fellow
mathematicians in 1980 and aired our “dirty laundry” for the world,
earning him some praise but mostly scorn for revealing insider secrets.
His primary thesis (across several books, but most clearly in *The Loss
of Certainty*) was that mathematics lost its way in the early twentieth
century, by dividing into two categories – Pure and Applied mathematics
– and divorcing great research from the traditional fount of all ideas:
real world problems, a.k.a. science.

Kline’s argument stands well on his own line of reasoning, but it also
appears validated in the attempts to spread mathematics at the popular
level. A brief survey of coffee-table books on mathematics shows that a
clear majority are written by non-mathematicians. Physicists and other
scientists seem more interested in disseminating interest in mathematics
among the general populace than mathematicians do. TED talks on
mathematics are typically given by teachers of other subjects. Sal Khan
is an engineer by training. By and large, the line between pure and
applied mathematics is the line between those who write on topics no one
else reads, and those involved with the greater enterprise of human
knowledge.

Mathematics (when it does play nice and share its toys with the other
children) is referred to as part of the acronym S.T.E.M.: science,
technology, engineering, and mathematics. This order facilitates a
pronounceable word, though the order in the mind of practitioners is:
mathematics, science, engineering, technology. Even here, mathematics is
somehow purer and when alloyed with the impure material universe it
leads to science (originally physics, then chemistry, and biology).
Science is the discovery of new natural laws, whereas the application to
routine situations is engineering. Most degenerately, the use of someone
else’s engineering products is relegated to technologists (Cat). Thus,
mathematics is “higher” and “nobler”, until it is sullied by descending
from the world of Forms into this physical world.

Kline maintained that mathematics has cut itself off from the source of
all its inspiration and greatness. The greatest mathematicians of the
past were concerned with solving real-world problems, from Newton to
Archimedes to Gauss. Even something as rarified as Galois Theory is only
one step removed from practical concerns and the production of
“technology” in the form of formulas. (Galois Theory is used to prove
that while there is a Quadratic Formula, a Cubic Formula, and even an
unwieldy Quartic Formula, there can never be a Quintic Formula.) Kline
argued that the reason mathematicians cannot come up with definition of
their field is because they have cleft themselves in twain, separating
one essential part of the disciple from the rest of itself.

## Back to School

The average reader is utterly perplexed by all these consternations of
the philosopher of mathematics: everyone knows what math is! Thanks to
public, government education, nearly every American has enough
experience with mathematics to know what it is, and that they don’t like
it! This misery is only compounded by the attempts of educational policy
makers to alter the definition. The intrusion of the philosophers into
American public life in the last generation was wonderfully illustrated
by Bob Parr, a.k.a. Mr. Incredible, in the movie *Incredibles 2*: “Why
would they change math?! Math is math! MATH is MATH!!” The New Math of
the 1960s – a tumultuous turn of events echoed in our own day with
Common Core and other standards – attempted to alter the way mathematics
is taught, but failed to dislodge the popular notions of arithmetic and
calculus as what math is.

Kline also ably critiqued the New Math. In his 1973 *Why Johnny Can't
Add: The Failure of the New Math*, and then in his even more scathing
1977 *Why the Professor Can’t Teach: The Dilemma of University
Education* he highlighted the disastrous turns of the American public
education system over the last 50 or 60 years. He argued there were
three important roles in education institutions: researchers who pushed
the boundaries of knowledge and experience; disseminators who
cross-pollinated research and prevented over-specialization by
connecting topics; and teachers/educators who were there to helping
people come into a knowledge of new and/or difficult subjects. Because
of various influences, research has been elevated to the *summum bonum*,
disseminators are all but gone, and any educators naïve enough to get
into high education, are driven from their field in utter frustration
(see the works of John Gatto).

## Recovery

Fortunately, Christians have not been entirely idle for the last half
century. A movement has been gathering steams both inside and outside
the Church, known as Classical Education, or more narrowly Classical
Christian Education. Dorothy L. Sayers’ essay, *The Lost Tools of
Learning*, has sparked a rebirth of interest which has grown into a
movement of millions across the United States and the world. As the
government schools descend deeper into mediocrity and debauchery, an
explosion of new schools and models of homeschool have proliferated,
with ensuing curriculum.

Many subjects have seen the development of entirely new curriculum,
written from a Christian perspective. Of course, these are sometimes
sloppy, knee-jerk, fundamentalist responses. But as the number of
second-generation Classically educated citizens grows, thoughtful and
well-reasoned textbooks are proliferating. Strangely, mathematics seems
not to have benefited from this renaissance. Mathematics is the subject
mostly likely to be abandoned and left to the methods of the last
century, because no one knows what a Christian view of mathematics would
be. The time is ripe for a reintegration of mathematics into the warp
and woof of Classical education.

“The Christian, therefore, cannot be indifferent to any branch of
earnest human endeavor. It must all be brought into *some* relation to
the gospel” (Wilson 38, quoting J. Gresham Machen). We maintain that
ancient and medieval people skillfully wove mathematics throughout their
education, indeed into their few of knowledge and the human enterprise
itself. We will argue that mathematics begins in the trivium, blossoms
in the quadrivium, and is the method of all the science going forward
into all arena of thought.

## Seven Liberal Arts

The trivium is an education paradigm which says everything must be
learned in three stages: grammar, logic, and rhetoric. Grammar means the
fundamental facts and data of the subject. Logic refers to the inner
workings of the disciples, how the facts fit together and are actually
used. Rhetoric is the presentation of the information learned, a
restatement of the subject by the learner. Modern Classical schools
often correlate these three aspects of learning (anachronistically) to
the modern age groupings of elementary school, middle school, and high
school (Perrin).

In his book, *Beauty for Truth’s Sake: On the Re-enchantment of
Education*, Stratford Caldecott says that the “four disciples of the
*quadrivium* – arithmetic, music, geometry, and astronomy – had one
thing in common: they were based in mathematics” (53). He maintains that
the subjects should be arranged in a grid, as follows:

| Arithmetic | Geometry  |
|------------|-----------|
| Music      | Astronomy |

This is because arithmetic comes first, the study of numbers. Next, we
add extension in **space**, to achieve geometry. Alternatively, we might
add **time** to number, and get music. Lastly, we could add **both**,
which was called astronomy. These subjects were originally much broader
in scope than today’s disciples of the same names. And yet, very few
musicians today learn that a perfect fifth is conceived of in the ear as
a ratio of 2:3, or that our equally-tempered instruments instead render
it as 1:$`\sqrt[12]{2^{7}}`$, or approximately 1.498.

Consider the role mathematics would play if one were educated in this
system from early childhood on. The times tables, area and volume
formulae, and definitions of various mathematical laws would be
memorized at the grammar stage. The ability to form conjectures and
begin to reason would be the focus of the logic or dialectic stage. “Is
an odd number to an odd power necessarily odd?” Able presentations and
defenses would predominate in the rhetoric stage. Mathematics class
would learn things such as various number-bases, and show that they
truly mastered the material by giving talks on which number base was the
best and why. A final exam might be a debate on the existence or
necessity of infinitesimals. The entire point of education would be how
to learn, not what to learn.

Not all people need education beyond the trivium. For those that do, the
quadrivium is again tutelage in *how*, with the *what* mattering much
less. Not many of us care about algebra for its own sake, but it tutors
us in how even such an abstract idea as number is subject to further
abstraction. Next, geometry is practice for epistemology. In the Middle
Ages, humanists said perspective drawings gave the correct perspective
on human being, an argument about anthropology. An educated person must
move past memorizing Euclid’s Postulates to debating **seeing**. Music
is an exercise in harmony, the inescapable humanness of our sensations
of the world, and yet the self-evident way the same Designer has made
the world and us. Natural objects vibrate at three and five times the
base frequency, so perfect fifths and major thirds are consonant to us
(and nature). The music of the spheres is the last stage. The ancient
knew that understanding the heavens is part and parcel of understanding
ourselves.

The idea that the “mathematicalization” of a subject means the mastery
of it have persisted into the present, secular educational system. One
cannot get a PhD in any subject with proving a hypothesis with
statistical data. In order to be a science, the practitioner “must seek
*mathematical* description rather than physical explanation” (Kline 60,
emphasis added). The world changed in Newton’s day from seeing the
worlds as merely *described* by mathematics, to a world *explained* by
mathematics (Barr, 40-41). Numerical knowledge is the higher form of
understanding, yet the manipulation of these numbers is an arcane magic,
reserved for the few. Expert testimony should still be subject to debate
and reason, a practice that is discouraged by our current educational
system.

## Ethics in Mathematics

In many ways, it is precisely the mathematicalization of everything that
has led to the conundrum in which we find ourselves. Descartian dualism
is the preeminent ontology of our time. Modern people think the world is
made up of self-evident truths, to which we apply the infallible methods
like mathematical reasoning, and out pops the burgeons bowers of truth
and knowledge. Is a return to math and numbers simply a return to the
start of process of secularization? An example from Scripture will show
us how to avoid that fate.

In John 6, the apostle whom Jesus loved records the feeding of the
5,000. Verse six says Jesus tested them with questions about where the
food would come from to feed so many. Philip answers with numbers,
forecasts of the costs of buying bread. Andrew answers with a numerical
inventory of the supplies on hand: 5 loaves and 2 fish. Jesus gave
thanks and distributed the food. In verse 13, the leftovers are counted:
12 baskets. Clearly, numbers (and possibly mathematics) can’t be all
bad. Examples could be multiplied, such as the triangular numbers of
John 21:11 or Acts 27:37.

The modern world has reverted to the methods of Philip and Andrew. We
are attempting to give a naturalistic and numeric account of the world,
and we are finding it extremely lacking. And yet, Jesus does not banish
numbers and accounting from his answer to human problems. Instead,
numbers come back into the equation only with the meaning they are given
by God and his word. Twelve is a highly symbolic number in the Bible. As
James Jordan describes *in Through New Eyes*, we must not impose our
meta-narrative onto the world, but instead take God’s given metaphors as
the keys to meaning and value.

Part of the success of Western Civilization has been due to the
scientific worldview. The idea is that our rational God has made the
world rational, and we can think his thoughts after him, that is, figure
out the world. Other civilizations have had more momentous discoveries
than we, and those much earlier than ours. The secret of the West lies
in the values, that our insights into Creation will endure forever, and
are part of the treasury of spoils which our God intends for us to
discover. The world is basically one big Easter egg hunt.

Readers of the Bible are often confused on page two: What is the point
of Genesis 2:10-14 in its context? We would do well to sing the song
*Into the World* by Jamie Soles, and consider that God has littered the
world with buried treasures. Some of these treasures are gold and
bdellium. Others are the Pythagorean Theorem and Fermat’s Last Theorem
(now Andrew Wile’s Theorem). This means some of the buried treasure is
between our ears!

Never has there been a time in the history of the world where more
people are engaged in mathematics, either per capita or raw numbers.

Educators should encourage growth; know the basics; provide
opportunities for repetition and rigor; replicate culture (CC 55).

Education is about truth, goodness, and beauty, over against the new,
the profitable, and the useful.(CC 19)
