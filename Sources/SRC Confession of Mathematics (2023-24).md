# Confession of Mathematics

Arithmetic
Mathematics is the science of numbers, shape, change, and the mathematical tools themselves.  This last item is self-referential, meaning that, in a certain sense, mathematics is the process of abstraction itself.  Alternatively, as Poincaré said, “Mathematics is the art of giving the same name to different things.”
The first idea of mathematics is in the quantification of things.  This is the original abstraction from which all others flow.  Almost all other features of the things being quantified are willfully ignored when we use the term “equal” to denote quantities which are the same.  “Equal” in mathematics is not like “equal” in normal speech.
The historical development of mathematics is mirrored in the individual processes of learning mathematics, and therefore ought to be followed, unless there are very compelling reasons not to.  Like other topics that touch the transcendentals – art, philosophy, etc. – mathematics has a history that cannot be safely ignored.
Human beings create bijections (one-to-one maps) between quantities of physical objects.  This is called ‘matching’, and is more fundamental than numbering.  Next, quantitates of physical objects are matched to an individual’s body parts, most commonly the fingers.  Finally, by either carving or writing, quantities are matched to tally marks.
Large amounts of tally marks quickly become unwieldy, and are frequently grouped into consistent bundles.  By abstraction, these bundles may be bundled into bundles of bundles, and so on and so forth.  This is then called a Number Base.  The most common number base is ten, but two, eight, sixteen, and sixty should also be learned.
Various procedures may be done to two quantities at a time.  These procedures are called Operations.  The most basic operation is Concatenation or running-together, to simply append one quantity to another.  This operation ought to be taught and distinguished, as evidenced by the desire of young students to think 2 + 3 = 23.  They are not wrong with respect to tally marks!
Two quantities of physical objects can be concatenated without respect to order.  This property is called Commutativity. 
A set of three quantities can be concatenated either beginning with the first two and then the third, or beginning with the second and third and then the first.  This is called Associativity.
The Counting Numbers are ordered, that is, each has a successor.  We take it on faith that each and every number has a successor, which cannot be proven.  As finite creatures, we cannot count beyond some (very large) number.  Repeated Succession is called Addition.  Addition is commutative and associative.
As people sought to count land, they discovered that area is the equivalent to Repeated Addition, a.k.a. Multiplication.  This equivalence between a concept from one area of mathematics and another is called Duality.
Multiplication is commutative and associative.
The process of undoing or reversing an operation is called its Inverse.  The inverse of addition is called Subtraction.  The inverse of Multiplication is called Division.  With respect to the Counting Numbers, many problems which can be posed using subtraction or division do not have answers.
Neither subtraction nor division is not commutative. 
The useful of mathematics in solving real-world problems leaves some incredulous.  The solution to this mystery is that our mental processes for doing mathematics have a common origin with the processes of the natural and mechanical world.
The many unsolvable problems of subtraction within the domain of Natural Numbers leads to further abstraction of the concept of Number.  The invention of a new object which has many of the same properties as the Natural Numbers arose as the answer to a number subtracted from itself.  This new number is called Zero, and is distinguished from “impossible”.  As a quantity, it would be a lack of something, but is sometimes more complicated than that.
We have already said that multiplication corresponds to the area of a rectangle.  Human beings know simpler objects – namely triangles – which have only three sides.  Their areas are related to quadrilaterals very simply.
A common shape in nature is the circle or sphere.  We call the ratio of the circumference of a circle to its diameter π (pi), or the ratio of the circumference to the radius τ (tau).
Human beings are incapable of exhaustively exhibiting the value of π.  We can describe many processes which better and better approximate it, seemingly without end.  However, no real-world application requires exhaustive knowledge.
Contemplation of the counting numbers shows that some of them arise in area computations (i.e. multiplication) and some do not.  We call these possible rectangular numbers Composite, and the non-rectangular ones Prime.  It can be shown that the Prime and Composites continue without conceivable ending, just as the Counting Numbers do.
When the Counting Numbers are considered in conjunction with zero, we call that the Whole Numbers.
If one of the successors to a given number is subtracted from it, neither the Counting nor the Whole numbers can describe such a quantity.  Human beings therefore invented predecessors to zero, called the Negative Numbers.  Just as the Counting Numbers have successors without conceivable end, these new numbers called Integers are believed to have predecessors without end.  These have real-world analogues in things like debt or reverse directions.
With respect to Counting Numbers, unity is first, and are the rest all ordered in proper fashion after it.  The Whole Numbers are said to begin at zero and proceed in the same way.  The Integers do not have a beginning, but a center, namely zero.  We therefore call zero the Identity of Addition, because adding zero to any number leaves it identical.
Within the integers, every number has an Additive Inverse, or Opposite, which can be added to it to produce the identity.
The conception of zero as the identity of addition leads to the conception of unity as the Identity of Multiplication.  That is, multiplying by unity leaves a number identical.  Within the Counting Numbers and the Integers, only unity has a multiplicative inverse, namely itself.
The interest in Multiplicative Inverses prompts the creation of another kind of number, namely the Rational Numbers, which are ratios of integers.  These have real-world analogues in fractions or equal divisions of physical objects.
With the creation of Rational Numbers, we are again confronted by our inabilities in the material world.  Without imagining we can complete unending processes, most numbers cannot divide.  If we allow for error, a cake can be divided into eleven, approximately equal pieces.  But a dollar cannot be shared equally among three friends.  Historically, the Babylonians said only a multiple of seven can be divided by seven.
Within the Rational Numbers, every number has a multiplicative inverse, except zero. 
Contemplation of Multiplicative Inverses and the Multiplicative Identity leads to an alternative organization.  Rather than zero being the center – as in the Integers, one can organize the Rational Numbers with unity as the center.  Dependent upon what one is multiplying by, there would be innumerable such arrangements of the Rationals.
Take for example one such arrangement of the Rationals: 1, 4, 16, 64, 256, 1024…. It is convenience to name the fact that each successor here is simply one more multiple of four.  We therefore call repeated multiplication Exponentiation, written with smaller numbers in superscript indicating the number of multiplications to do.  e.g. 4*4*4*4*4=1024=45.
Such an arrangement and relabeling of the “family of fours” among the Rational would have integer exponents, i.e. 40, 41, 42, .…  For any such family, the zeroth term will always be equal to unity.
Just as we expanded the integers to the Rationals for ordinary numbers, we might try to expand exponents to include rational numbers too.  Sometimes, such a procedure will work, such as in 41/2=2.  However, much more often, such a process will prove impossible to complete fully.
There is a duality between a number to a 1/2 exponent and a feature of squares.  For example, a square with area of 25 has sides of 5, and 251/2=5.  We therefore give the name of “square root” to the exponent of 1/2.  A 1/3 exponent is called a cube root, after the three dimensional equivalent.
The inverse of exponentiation is called Logarithms.  There are, therefore, three ways to write every mathematical sentence involving exponents.  e.g. 23=8, log28=3 and ∛8=2 are all the same arithmetic sentence.
Letters are often used for a variety of purposes in mathematical notation.  First, they may be used to hold the place for an unknown, a quantity to be found.  For example, x+3=7 is an equation where x can only take on the value of 4.  Second, they may be used for variables, where a range of values all work and no one answer is expended.  For example, the equation 2x-4y=8 has an uncountable number of x and y combinations that satisfy it, though not every combination works.  Third, they may be used for functions, processes that map inputs to outputs.  For example, f(x)=3x-7 is another straight line (as a graph), where f is the name of the linear process that turns 1 into -4 and 9 into 20.  There are other uses too, and sometimes letters from other languages are used.
Complicated squares and square roots (called conics) are a much more convoluted topic than human beings are capable of solving exhausting.  We are, however, able to describe unending processes which get better and better for answering such questions, though they are never concluded.  Methods of such processes for cubes and cube roots (called elliptic curves) is a current area of interest and research.
In the sixteenth century, it became desirable to find square roots of negative numbers, in order to solve real-world problems.  This lead to the invention of a new kind of number, namely that √-1 is called i (or sometimes j).  i2=-1.  This number is not unique.  Multiples of this new number are called Imaginary (or Lateral) Numbers.  Sums of these new numbers and previously extant numbers are called Complex Numbers.
It is expedient to imagine numbers as laid out in a linear fashion.  For the integers, zero is in the middle, the positives stretch out to the right, the negatives out to the left.  The Lateral numbers would extend up and down in such a schema.
In the seventeenth century, Isaac Newton and Gottfried Leibniz took on the task of quantifying change.  Such a process would require there to be a successor to zero, a task possible within the Integers, Whole Numbers, and Counting Numbers, but impossible with Rational Numbers.  Once again, the invention of numbers “solved” a real-world dilemma.  The notations of this new number vary considerably, but we shall call it ε (epsilon).  Epsilon is also called an infinitesimal, and has the property that ε2=0.  Zero is an infinitesimal, but we posit that there are others.  We are incapable of distinguishing among infinitesimals, except to say one is zero and that there are others.
The capacity to have a successor to zero allows to the description of instantaneous rates of change, an otherwise incoherent concept.  This new branch of mathematics is called differential calculus.
