> [!info] Source snapshot
> Murphy-authored essay; earliest full statement in the corpus. Redemptive-historical (Creation / Rebellion / Redemption / Consummation), Kline, triperspectivalism, Wildberger and Robinson as allies. Footnotes preserved at end. See [[Theology of Number]] and [[Timeline of the Position]].

In this paper, I shall attempt to give a brief overview of Christian
Mathematics. I shall begin with the known truths from Holy Scripture,
contrast the false narratives of the Flesh, and endeavor to express the
rich call of God to mathematical rectitude. Mathematics is defined in a
two-fold way. First, it is a method, namely quantitative reasoning.
Second, it is a science. I shall proceed in a Redemptive-Historical
fashion, but I shall spend considerable time dedicated to the
educational method under which mathematics should be promulgated and
conducted.

Creation

We begin with the self-disclosing God of the Bible. The LORD has made
himself known under the triune name of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit[^1].
Nevertheless, there is but One God[^2]. And so, we are immediately
confronted with a numerical paradox[^3]. Insofar as mathematics involves
the wisdom of numbers, this is a mathematical enigma. We possess
knowledge analogical to God’s own idea of
Oneness, and knowledge analogical to his idea of Threeness, but we are
incapable of reconciling the two into a coherent unity. Thus, we see
that we must begin with epistemological humility.

Numbers play an important role throughout the opening pages of the
Bible. During the creation week, the days are enumerated (except the
seventh), and the Dominion Mandate is given to human beings to go forth
and multiply[^4]. As many authors have noted, Genesis 1:27 skillfully
oscillates between singular humanity, and the plurality of men and
women. This is confirmed by Moses in 2:24, where human couples mirror
God’s trinity in a kind of
“binity”: the obvious two are called
“one flesh.” Every human language has the
distinction called *grammatical number* in some way, further indicating
the inseparable nature of number from our thinking.

The persons and personhood of God and Man can be described using the
language of numbers, but we also see many of
Yahweh’s attributes that cannot be enumerated.
God’s miracles are without number (Job 5:9). His
understanding is without measure (Psalm 147:5). He has neither beginning
nor ending (Psalm 90:2). We are incapable of exhaustively understanding
his ways (Romans 11:33). To quote the Shorter Catechism,
“God is a Spirit, infinite, eternal, and
unchangeable, in his being, wisdom, power, holiness, justice, goodness,
and truth.” We see, therefore, that just as numerability is capable of
showing us the comprehensibility and incomprehensibility of God, so too
is innumerability.

Statements about particular numerical facts or arithmetic laws are
*words*, and speech is a communicated divine attribute, given to Man in
his God-like task of dominion over the world. God rules the world by the
word of his power (Hebrew 1:3) and he has given that same prerogative to
human beings. Mathematical axioms are statements about the character of
God. We need not separate the abstraction from the instantiation. There
is a real and important leap from “two apples and
two apples make four apples” to 2+2=4, but
“because of the equal ultimacy of the one and the
many, the abstraction and its embodiments belong together, each helping
to define the other”[^5]. Distinctions, sameness, and the rules that
govern them are descriptions of the character of God.

We find the same level of cohesion when we look at nature. Scientific
formulae are capable of accurately describing phenomena succinctly and
beautifully. There is a danger in calling them such things as The Law of
Special Relativity or Coulomb’s Law, namely, that
we might think them impersonal powers, non-personal objects akin to the
Force in *Star Wars*. Instead, we should name them
“God’s customary ways of
acting”, for he does not grow tired of repetition as we do[^6].

Indeed, the entire point of the natural realm is to indicate and glorify
the character of God. All things were created to reveal the attributes
of our Lord. We ourselves, as human beings, are made from nature, in the
image of God. Thus, we see a threefold relationship exists: that we
cannot learn about ourselves or nature, without learning about God, and
every other combination. As Calvin noted, all knowledge
“consists of two parts: the knowledge of God and
of ourselves” (35). All creation is a metaphorical language[^7], an
illustration of God’s character, and that goes
for scientific truths no less.

Rebellion

As is painfully obvious, we human do not live according to
God’s instruction, as described in Scripture. The
second phase of history is Rebellion, the miserable attempt of our first
father and mother to carve out their own domain in
God’s universe and have autonomy (Genesis 3).
This insurrection is played out in a myriad of ways every hour in human
affairs, as we suppress the truth in unrighteousness (Romans 1). Human
beings do not need to be told that God exists; we need to quit shoving
that fact out of our minds. This ongoing attempt to break up
God’s rule has implications in all human
endeavors, and mathematics is no exception.

Since the paradox of the Trinity requires that we mathematically bend
the knee to the LORD, it is among the first concepts jettisoned by the
unbeliever. The equal ultimacy of the one and the three is torn in two,
and one side dominates the other. In B.C. times, it was the Many-ness
which carried the field, and special revelation was required to express
the contrary. Archeologically, we see the evidence of the triumph of the
Many in Babylonian and Egyptian mathematics. Today we call that view
empiricism[^8].

We have an embarrassment of riches from the Ancient Near East. We know
more about the world of the Hebrew Bible than any generation before us.
There are thousands of tablets in Babylonian (Akkadian) waiting to be
translated, the backlog is so extensive. Nearly all of these are
receipts, numerical records of transactions over goods. We currently do
not know how they were able to achieve certain calculations which
resemble modern trigonometry, sequences and series, and calculus. All of
this was done before the invention of the number zero!

Today, “many-ness” is the common view of
mathematics from those outside the discipline. It is ensconced in the
acronym S.T.E.M., which stands for science, technology, engineering, and
mathematics. However, that order is not the logical one, but simply the
one that makes an English word. Hierarchically, the order proceeds thus:
mathematics, science, engineering, technology[^9], a progression from
the more rarified to the more useful. Consider the parabolic path of a
thrown object. Mathematics was used to create the Quadratic Formula,
capable of solving any equation of the form *y=ax<sup>2</sup>+bx+c*,
where *a*, *b*, and *c* are constant numbers. Science – specifically
physics – tells us that the flight-path of a thrown object on the
surface of the Earth over short distances most resembles a parabola.
Engineers know that such ball-tossing problems will be the same every
day, so the equations can be simplified. And thus, they are reducible to
the technology of an app on your phone which spits out the distance the
ball will go, if you input a few numbers. The goal is almost to make
mathematics safe, legal, and rare, because the payoff is in the myriad
details.

Besides the pleasure of calling most people Babylonians, this drastic
over-simplification also explains the experience of
“math people” when they encounter
“real math” for the first time. These individuals
are given advanced techniques, and are inexplicably able to do them at a
young age, unlike their peers. The technical, solution-based exercises
of Calculus I, Calculus II, Calculus III, and Differential Equations are
easy work for them. Suddenly and painfully, the detailed work done
without abstraction or argument turns into the proof-based classes of
Abstract Algebra and Complex Analysis. Perhaps the beleaguered student
was a mathematics major, but now questions “What
even **is** mathematics?” and scrambles to switch to a math education
degree or a more data-driven science, before they have to take Number
Theory!

The professional mathematicians fall away from the Trinity in the other
direction: unbalanced emphasis on oneness, called rationalism. For them,
**everything** should be simplifiable to just one equation. One could
hear that push in the clamor for the Grand Unified Theory of Quantum
Gravity. In most accounts of the history of mathematics, it is said not
to have really begun until the Greeks, and their insistence upon
deductive reasoning[^10]. Somehow, we find ourselves in possession of
universally true statements, which we combine to make fresh truths.
Modern mathematicians thought that if they turned the handle on that
crank long enough and well enough, they would out maneuver God, and
establish universal, ahistorical, unassailable truth without reference
to him. Needless to say, these efforts have collapsed spectacularly.

Morris Kline broke ranks with his fellow mathematicians and aired all
the dirty laundry in public, a deed which did not win him many fans. In
his *The Loss of Certainty*, he describes the
“cataclysm” after the proof of the validity of
non-Euclidean geometries. The holes were hastily patched, but they were
nothing compared to the “disastrous aftermath” of
Cantor’s theories on infinities. Cantor was a
Lutheran pastor’s son who was repeatedly institutionalized, and created many
irreconcilable axioms of mathematics. Two of the questions it raised, the
Continuum Hypothesis and the Axiom of Choice, were later shown to be
independent of Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory: Gödel showed that they cannot be
disproved from it, and Paul Cohen showed that they cannot be proved from it[^11].
David Hilbert (who set forth many of the parameters for defining
mathematics as **the** autonomous discipline) famously remarked,
“we do not wish to be expelled from the paradise
of Cantor’s theory of sets.” Mathematics was
proven to be a limited, human endeavor, without unassailable foundations
and not *a priori* fact.

Professional mathematicians and philosophers of mathematics continue to
vigorously bury their heads in the sand to this day. The various schools
of thought claim their fiefdoms and dare not engage each other in
debate, lest their common lack of foundation be exposed. Platonists,
Formalists, Intuitionists, and Set Theorists[^12] go right on proving
obscure facts that no one will ever use, in peer-reviewed journals that
no one else will ever (or can even) read. Pure mathematics is quickly
becoming the hard science version of lesbian dance theory[^13].

Redemption

How mathematics can be “baptized” is similar to
how any other field can be. A definition of
“Christian Plumbing” might appear to be tritely
different from Secular Plumbing, without the right perspective.
Theologians Vern Poythress and John Frame advocate taking a
tri-perspectival approach to all matters[^14]. This seems to be the best
attempt at regaining the paradoxical (yet not contradictory) approach of
Trinitarian theology. A good application of triperspectivalism to
mathematics is to consider it through the lenses of orthodoxy,
orthopraxy, and orthopathy. Mathematics is equally well-served by this
approach, because it is not merely truth, but good and beauty besides.

Orthodoxy is right-thinking, correct ideas. Of course, if done alone, it
is like a gold ring in a pig’s snout! Attempts to
answer “What is mathematics?” from a purely
abstract framework fall into this temptation. Orthopraxy is
right-practice, good actions. For that, we shall have to turn our
attention to education and scholarly institutions, where mathematics is
transmitted and carried out. Finally, orthopathy is right-emotions,
beautiful affections. We will return to this topic after discussing how
math is done.

The overwhelming majority of Westerners today have the bulk of their
ideas about mathematics and how it should be carried out from their time
at school. Again, Morris Kline is extremely helpful here. In his book,
*Why the Professor Can’t Teach*, he details three
“offices” which should exist in well-functioning
universities. The researcher pushes the frontiers of human knowledge,
though perhaps is unaware of much else. The disseminator or networker
understands and connects those on the bleeding-edge and cross-pollinates
ideas, preventing institutions from becoming inbred and insular.
Finally, the educator skillfully communicates difficult ideas to the
next generation of scholars. In a well-humming education system, these
three roles are in a proper balance.

In the aftermath of WWII, a series of historical events brought about a
nearly complete misalignment. American universities felt embarrassed by
their perceived quaintness and immaturity from the recent influx of
European research scientists. Researchers were promoted to the *summum
bonum* over all others. PhD programs almost exclusively aim at producing
them. Universities list only them as the feathers in their caps. They
decide policy and are the experts we interview in a crisis, despite
their colossal myopia. Disseminators are all but eradicated. Part of the
craze in keeping the cult of Richard Feynman going – despite him having
been a horrific misogynist and terrible philosopher – is that he was one
of the last, brilliant cross-pollinators. Educators still come along,
but typically quit in short order, as they experience endless rejection
and no power to speak of. For the high school educators perspective, see
the works of John Taylor Gatto.

As an aside, there are some bright spots, despite the pall cast over
American mathematics education. Jo Boaler’s
movement promotes deep understanding and good practices in math
education. MSRI at Berkeley promotes beauty in mathematics. Paul
Lockhart was not afraid to lambast the evils in mathematics education in
his *A Mathematician’s Lament* and followed it up
with a positive statement of what should be in his books *Measure* and
*Arithmetic*. Philips-Exeter boarding school in New England shares their
invaluable curriculum of six years’ worth of
problem sets. Even in chaos, pockets of common grace still exist among
the heathens.

Christian communities have slowly and unevenly begun to reject the
government schools. Kline describes the damage wrought by researchers
who implemented the New Math[^15] in his *Why Johnny
Can’t Add*. The reform movements that followed
– culminating in Common Core – have been an attempt to recover the
concepts and motivations behind basic mathematics, without the
underlying skills needed. (They are also being taught by math teachers
who don’t know those concepts for themselves.)
The knee-jerk reaction of many communities to retreat and use Saxon math
is a return to rote-skills, without in depth discussion of the concepts
or motivations.

If there is to be a clear understanding of what Christian Mathematics
is, it cannot take place merely in the realm of orthodoxy. Even attempts
at “Christianization” of the various schools of
thought with regards to philosophy of mathematics will always fall
short, unless such discussions also include orthopraxy. Indeed,
orthopathy is impossible without a rich
“liturgical” practice of righteous living. This
right practice shall be presented as Classical Education, with the
defense of it left to others.

## Redemption’s Method: Classical Education

The last two generations have seen a renaissance of evangelical
homeschooling and private Christian schools. Dorothy Sayers pivotal
essay, *The Lost Tools of Learning*, has been followed by popularizing
and apologetic books by Douglas Wilson, David Hicks, and many others.
Christopher Perrin lays out the complete Classical paradigm – from
cradle to grave – in his *Introduction to Classical Education*. While
there are important differences from the medieval instantiation of the
program in the modern one, it can be broken down using the same, basic
terminology. Primary education is covered under the term **trivium**,
secondary education as the **quadrivium**, and graduate studies under
one of the four **sciences**. Classical education is many things, but
here let us focus on the method of teaching.

The trivium now roughly corresponds to elementary, middle, and high
school. However, the trivium is also the method by which all subjects
are learned. Taken as sequential phases, they are nonetheless always
present, but emphasized successively over one’s
childhood. The grammar stage is about learning the basic facts, the
vocabulary and memorizable rules of the subject. The logic or dialectic
stage is about understanding and reworking for
one’s self those same facts. Do they really work?
Why? How? Attempting to implement the brute facts once one has learned
them is hard work. Finally, the rhetoric stage is about presenting what
you have learned in persuasive speech. Teaching a subject, one must
learn is most deeply of all.

It is important to note here that mathematics practice in this country
is generally stuck at the grammar stage, even into the upperclassman
years of university. Our standardized tests expect students to merely
recall a series of formulas, and – at most – solve a few actual
problems. Have you ever seen a debate, a winsome presentation, or heard
of an oral exam on any math subject by a high school or college student?
American educators have long recognized that other countries out-perform
us by teaching less math topics per year[^16]. Other G8-country students
actually master the material before moving on, but we lack the
self-discipline to do less.

While the trivium model is being implemented in more and more places, a
lack of hand offs to quadrivium-model-institutions seems to be crippling
the later stages. In this author experience, many otherwise well-humming
classical schools lack a rhetorical phase only in their S.T.E.M. areas.
Could it be that we do not believe that mathematics is a liberal art
too? Or, have we not seen rational, passionate discourse on
science-topics in so long, we have forgotten what they look like? I
propose that we must recover the correct quadrivium model if we are to
see sufficient Christian Mathematics in practice.

In his book, *Beauty for Truth’s Sake: On the
Re-enchantment of Education*, Stratford Caldecott says that the
“four disciples of the *quadrivium* – arithmetic,
music, geometry, and astronomy – had one thing in common: they were
based in mathematics” (53). He maintains that the subjects should be
arranged in a grid, as follows:

|            |           |
|------------|-----------|
| Arithmetic | Geometry  |
| Music      | Astronomy |

This is because arithmetic comes first, the study of pure numbers. Next,
we add extension in **space**, to achieve geometry. Alternatively, we
might add **time** to number, and get music. Lastly, we could add
**both**, which is called astronomy. (These subjects were originally
much broader in scope than today’s disciplines of
the same names.) Ideally, each of these disciples is learned in the
trivium style, but then goes on to ask new questions.
“How does this disciple change how I view the
world? God? Myself? What is the nature of physical reality? The human
world? Truth? The divine life?”

As Caldecott says, all of the quadrivium is about **quantitative
reasoning**. For the unrighteous mind, this can lead to the fatuous
assumption that everything can be quantified. But as we saw from
Creation, this is self-evidently not the case, as we see modeled for us
by our God. Our unparalleled success as a civilization is the fruit of
the medieval and Renaissance worldview that God’s
Creation can be understood via the language of mathematics. We threaten
to ruin our success by totalizing that same mathematics, stripping the
dignity and uniqueness of human beings as image-bearers. Math is the
language of **things**. English (or Hebrew or Swahili) is the language
of **persons**. There are foundations which precede mathematics, both
logically and chronologically (in an education).

Definitions of mathematics which leave out orthopraxy suffer miss the
two-fold nature of the term. In a broad sense, mathematics is synonymous
with quantitative reasoning, the analysis and reduction of ideas to
rational, numerical descriptions. This is more of a method than any one
field of study. Second, in the narrow sense, mathematics is quantitative
reasoning about certain abstract objects, beginning with quantity
itself. Here, we can borrow from non-Christians[^17] such as Devlin, who
defines mathematics as the “science of patterns”
in “number, shape, motion, change, and space,
*and of the mathematical tools that are in this study* \[i.e. logic\]”
(3). Of course, we need to amend such definitions, that all that
“study” must be preceded by Biblical
presuppositions and structure of how such study is even possible, that
we are thinking God’s thoughts after him, as his
image bearers in his world. Thus, we see that there are two senses we
have been using the term **mathematics**.

The redemption of mathematics, then, can be delineated in several
components. No merely cerebral definition is enough: mathematics – like
all activities – must be conceived rightly, practiced rightly, and
experienced rightly. Second, it must be preceded by an ethical and
liberal framework, namely the Biblical worldview and morals. Third, we
must distinguish between mathematics as a method, and the various
disciplines under the banner of mathematics. (As an added bonus, this
clears up the perennial debate of the relationship between mathematics
and logic.) Finally, mathematics as a practice cannot be separated from
how it is taught, and that should be the trivium, followed by the
quadrivium.

Consummation

An enormous amount of work needs to be done before Christian Mathematics
can achieve a comfortable groove in this country, and be seen to stand
on its own two feet. In his excellent essay, *Worldview Test Case:
Christianity in Math Class*, Jim Nance lists several goals for a
Christian mathematics classroom. I will repeat them here with slight
alteration:

Mathematics describes the invisible attributes of God. We no longer look
for physical explanations[^18] of gravity, magnetisms, or gas pressure.
We are satisfied with *F=ma* and other formulae to describe these Laws.
Rather than seeing them as impersonal forces, we must define laws as
“the ways God customarily acts” and mathematical
laws as “descriptions of the internal coherence
and character of God”.

In describing the physical and invisible world, we are engaged in a form
of theology, because we and all nature are signs pointing towards God.
The world changed in Newton’s day from being
described by mathematics, to a world explained by mathematics[^19]. We
are not heathens dumbstruck by the accuracy of
Einstein’s equations to 12 decimal places, which
he came up with while sitting in a patent office in Bern. The same God
who made us made the world, and he has filled it with buried treasure
(Proverbs 25:2). Some of those treasures are literal jewels, others are
“natural laws” which we get to discover in the
physical world, and some are accessible by simply closing our eyes
and/or scribbling mathematics on the back of a napkin.

Teachers are modeling the transcendental virtues for their students,
positively by what they commend, and negatively by what they punish. All
of our lives are to be holy, pointing to what is good, true, and
beautiful. Mathematics is to be no different. It takes a great deal of
training to appreciate all of the beauty in a Rembrandt painting. Math
is also an art. Would that we could all bask in the wonder of
Euler’s equation e<sup>πi</sup>+1=0!

The trivium is a helpful rubric for stages of intellectual development,
but it must be practiced at all times. “What are
the fundamentals of a subject? Can you implement them? Now explain to me
like I was a five-year-old.” Too much math is done without any view
toward those who come after us. It is an elitist club, where
practitioners perpetuate the hard way, because it was done to them.
Trauma begets trauma. If we really believed this world is Our
Father’s, full of wonder and gifts liberally
sprinkled all around, we would want to spread it as far and wide and to
as many people as possible. The rhetoric stage needs re-emphasizing.

Problems-based instruction is the only way that preserves the dignity of
the student. After the grammar has been covered, I insult you as an
image-bearer if I deny you the chance to wrestle with the material
yourself. What would we think of an art teacher who took the brush from
the hand of her students, or never gave them time to make mistakes?
Mathematics teachers must do the same.

Knowledge without love is worse than an out-of-tune gong. Do we love our
neighbors? Do we love mathematics? It might be necessary from time to
time to have some things done out of necessity, but it is generally to
be avoided. Perhaps math is just a stepping stone toward one of the
sciences for some. But wherever we are, if it is done without love, it
is detrimental to our walk with God. Obedience breeds joy.

One topic not mentioned by Nance is that mathematics education is
greatly in need of breadth. Since the space race against the Soviets,
math has been a death march to calculus, despite the great need for
geometers, topologists, and number theorists. Why is that? Analysis is a
fine field of study, but why can’t we discuss
modular arithmetic starting in elementary school? Why are we done with
geometry in ninth grade, never to return for amazing, advanced topics,
such as inversion? If education is about *how* to learn, not *what* to
learn, math class might never be the same twice.

There are whole fields of study which Christian mathematicians should be
much more interested in than their heathen counterparts. Normal J.
Wildberger[^20] has not received nearly enough attention as he deserves
in this respect. His works include Rational Trigonometry, Algebraic
Calculus, and the retooling of elementary mathematics with Linear
Algebra as the foundation. The godless hubris of the so-called
Enlightenment may be pointed out in many Christian Western Civilization
classes, but it is mindlessly preached down the hall in the Calculus
classroom. Newton and Leibnitz’s original concept
was superseded by the decades later idea of limits. Abraham
Robinson’s work[^21] with infinitesimals is an
excellent example of rediscovering the intuitive origins of calculus.

Finally, the triperspectival work of Frame and Poythress should inform
any Christian mathematician’s approach, either as
an educator or in a specific sub-discipline. We should be careful of the
modern, secular schools of thought in mathematics, and not
over-emphasize any of the normative, situational, or existential
perspectives. Many math teachers got into the job because they want to
preach only the normative!

I cannot fully imagine what the orthopathy of Christian Mathematics will
be. As a mathematician, the number of laws and facts I
“know” far outstrips what I can
“do”, which far outstrips what I can
“feel”. Sometimes, I glimpse the wonder of
uncovering something new, no one has seen before, and I madly practice
it, until I am fluent in it, like a new-found language. I feel a peace
knowing God buried that jewel in the world for me to find and share with
others, benefitting all our lives forever. Every good thing will be
carried forward into eternity, part of our offering back to God the
bounty he has given us. Praise the LORD!
