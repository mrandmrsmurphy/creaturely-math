# Slope Approximations

Many existing trigonometry problems state a wedge in degrees. To use them in constructible trigonometry, replace the wedge by a **nearby rational slope**. This table gives a short rational for each wedge from $1^\circ$ to $45^\circ$. The wedge is written as $n/360$ of a turn, with $n^\circ$ for readers coming from other frameworks.

Five rows are **exact**: those wedges have slopes in $\mathbb K$ with short closed forms ([[Exact Slopes]]). The other forty are approximations.

## How good are they

Over the forty approximate rows the **relative error in the slope is at most 0.36%** (at $16^\circ$, $-0.36\%$; then $28^\circ$, $+0.31\%$), and the wedge a slope actually measures differs from its target by at most $0.072^\circ$ (at $28^\circ$). Small wedges have small slopes, so a small absolute error is a large relative one; the $1^\circ$ and $3^\circ$ rows are therefore given to higher accuracy.

- **Wedges that are multiples of $3^\circ$** ($3^\circ,6^\circ,9^\circ,\dots$) also have exact slopes in $\mathbb K$, on [[Exact Slopes]]. The rational here is for hand work.
- **Wedges that are not** ($1^\circ,2^\circ,4^\circ,\dots$) have **no exact slope in $\mathbb K$**. A wedge of $n/360$ of a turn has a slope in $\mathbb K$ only when the regular polygon with that many sides is constructible, and $360$ is divisible by $9$, so $1^\circ$ is not. A rational approximation is the honest thing to write (the standard theory of constructible polygons, to check).
- **Method.** Each rational is a **convergent or semiconvergent** of the continued fraction of the exact slope (checked for every row), chosen with a short denominator. $\tfrac7{401}$ for $1^\circ$ is a convergent with a longer denominator (the short alternative $\tfrac3{172}$ is $-0.076\%$). The $3^\circ$ row is the exception: $\tfrac{\sqrt2}{27}$ is a single radical in $\mathbb K$ chosen because it beats every rational below denominator $230$ (the exact slope of $3^\circ$ is a nest on [[Exact Slopes]]).
- **Improving one.** The sum of two wedges is exact by the addition law ([[Sum of Slopes]]). For example the $1^\circ$ wedge is the $3^\circ$ wedge plus the negative of the $2^\circ$ wedge, so an exact slope for $3^\circ$ and a rational for $2^\circ$ give a slope for $1^\circ$ whose only inexactness is the rational for $2^\circ$.
