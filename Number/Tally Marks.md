# Tally Marks

The first stage of the history of number, and the place [[Historical Order]] begins. Murphy's observation: **before positional numerals there were tally marks**, and the four operations had a plain meaning there:

| Operation | In tally marks |
|---|---|
| **Addition** | **concatenation**: write one tally after the other |
| **Subtraction** | **removal**: cross off marks |
| **Multiplication** | **area** (an array of rows and columns) or **repeated addition** |
| **Division** | sharing out, or grouping: how many equal piles |

Positional numbers are already taught; the point is to **acknowledge the phase before them**, and to keep tally marks and concatenation as part of early arithmetic so that the later notation is seen as a *compression* of something the student can still unfold ([[Historical Order]], the transparency test).

## The history, in brief (all to check)

- **Notched bones.** The Lebombo bone (southern Africa, c. 43,000 years ago, about $29$ notches) and the Ishango bone (central Africa, c. 20,000 years ago) are the usual early evidence of tallying; what the notches meant (counting, a lunar record, something else) is debated.
- **Tokens and tablets.** Clay tokens, one per counted thing, preceded written numerals in Mesopotamia (Schmandt-Besserat; contested).
- **Additive numerals.** Egyptian hieroglyphic numerals repeat a symbol for each unit, ten, hundred, and so on; Roman numerals are additive with a subtractive shortcut ($IV$, $IX$). Both are tallies with symbols for bundles.
- **Bundling into a base.** The step to positional notation is *bundling bundles*: the Babylonians' $60$, our ten ([[Counting and Bases]], [[Sexagesimal]]). Place value, with a symbol for an empty place, is later.

## What the operations look like on tallies

**Addition.** $\;\mathbf{||||}+\mathbf{|||}=\mathbf{|||||||}$: put the strings together. Counting the result is a separate act. Grouping in fives, $7=1\cdot5+2$ (checked), is the first **carry**: a full bundle is replaced by one mark of a higher kind.

**Subtraction.** Cross off. It can *fail*: with three marks one cannot remove five ($3-5=-2$). That failure is the historical motive for new kinds of number ([[Counting and Bases]], step 6), and the later **borrow** in the written algorithm is the reverse of bundling: an unbundling.

**Multiplication.** A $3$ by $4$ array of marks counts $12$ whichever way it is read, so $3\times4=4\times3$ is *seen* (the commutativity of [[Historical Order]]'s cardboard is already here). As repeated addition, $13\times11$ can be done by **doubling and adding**, the Egyptian method: double $13$ to $26,52,104$; since $11=8+2+1$, add $104+26+13=143$ (checked). It uses only concatenation and doubling, and it is binary in disguise ([[Binary]]).

**Division.** Deal marks into equal piles; what is left over is the remainder, the seed of clock math ([[Modular Arithmetic]]).

## What concatenation teaches

Putting strings together is **associative** (the grouping does not matter) and, as counts, **commutative**. The empty string is the identity. Tally marks under concatenation are the simplest example of the structures of [[Groups]]: the counting numbers are exactly the strings on one symbol, a monoid. A child sees associativity by gluing three strips of marks before the word exists.

## Tally marks and the vault's position

A tally is a **certificate in its simplest form**: one mark per thing, the count *exhibited*, equality checked by matching ([[Certificates]]). And it makes the **notation-versus-number** point concrete ([[Creational Ultrafinitism]]):

| Number | tally marks | positional digits |
|---|---|---|
| $1{,}000$ | $1{,}000$ | $4$ |
| $10^6$ | $10^6$ | $7$ |
| $10^{200}$ | $10^{200}$ | $201$ |

The positional numeral is a **logarithmic compression** of the tally. A tally of $10^{200}$ marks cannot be made (no universe holds it), yet the numeral takes $201$ digits. What the numeral lets one *write* is not what one can *hold*; the tally is the form in which the number would be held. That is the difference between a notation and a number, taught at the bottom of the curriculum.

## For teaching

1. **Keep tallies in the first year**, with groups of five (the "gate"), and let students add by joining and subtract by crossing off.
2. **Introduce the bundle** (a mark for five, then ten) *before* place value, so the carry is a bundle and the borrow an unbundle.
3. **Arrays for multiplication** before the times table, with doubling as an early algorithm.
4. **Say out loud** that positional notation came later and is a compression: the phase before it is part of the story, not a stage to be forgotten.

## Hard problems

1. **The archaeology is contested.** What the notches and tokens meant is not settled; the page claims only the pattern of operations.
2. **Whether tallies slow fluency.** The goal is total fluency in the four operations by grade 5 ([[Historical Order]]); how long to keep tallies before moving to the written algorithm is a classroom question.
3. **Zero.** A tally has no mark for nothing; zero belongs to the positional stage ([[Counting and Bases]]).
