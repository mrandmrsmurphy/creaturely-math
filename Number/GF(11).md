# GF(11)

The field with eleven elements: the integers mod $11$ ([[Modular Arithmetic]]) with all four operations. Same treatment as [[GF(3)]], [[GF(5)]] and [[GF(7)]], without the quadratic extension.

**Balanced residues.** The eleven elements are written $\bar5,\bar4,\bar3,\bar2,\bar1,0,1,2,3,4,5$, where $\bar5=-5\equiv6$, $\bar4\equiv7$, $\bar3\equiv8$, $\bar2\equiv9$, $\bar1\equiv10$. Negation swaps the bar and the unbarred digit.

*[AI]* Every table, factorization and count on this page was computed and verified exactly.

- **Fermat:** $x^{10}=1$ for every $x\ne0$, and $x^{11}=x$ for every $x$.
- **Orders:** $1$ has order $1$; $\bar1$ has order $2$; $3,4,5,\bar2$ have order $5$; and $2,\bar3,\bar4,\bar5$ have order $10$. So the **primitive roots** are $2,\bar3,\bar4,\bar5$. They come in inverse pairs: $2\leftrightarrow\bar5$ and $\bar3\leftrightarrow\bar4$.
- **Discrete logarithm base $2$:** $2^0,\dots,2^9$ are $1,2,4,\bar3,5,\bar1,\bar2,\bar4,3,\bar5$. So $\log_2$ of $1,2,3,4,5,\bar1,\bar2,\bar3,\bar4,\bar5$ is $0,1,8,2,4,5,6,3,7,9$ (mod $10$).

## Squares and roots of unity

- **Squares:** $0,\ 1,\ 3,\ 4,\ 5,\ \bar2$. The five nonzero squares are the elements of order dividing $5$, and the nonsquares are $\bar1,2,\bar3,\bar4,\bar5$. Square roots: $\sqrt1=\pm1$, $\sqrt3=\pm5$, $\sqrt4=\pm2$, $\sqrt5=\pm4$, $\sqrt{\bar2}=\pm3$.
- **$\bar1$ is not a square** ($11\equiv3\bmod4$): the fiat number $i$ adjoins a new field, as in $\mathrm{GF}(3)$ and $\mathrm{GF}(7)$.
- **Fifth roots of unity** exist ($5\mid10$): $x^5=1$ has the roots $1,3,4,5,\bar2$, so $x^5-1=(x-1)(x-3)(x-4)(x-5)(x+2)$ and $x^4+x^3+x^2+x+1$ splits completely. Likewise $x^5+1=(x+1)(x+3)(x+4)(x+5)(x-2)$, and the primitive tenth roots are $2,\bar3,\bar4,\bar5$.
- **Cube roots of unity** do not exist ($3\nmid10$): $x^2+x+1$ is irreducible. And $x^2+1$ is irreducible.

## Polynomials over GF(11)

**Identities.** $x^{11}-x=\prod_a(x-a)$ and $x^{10}-1=\prod_{a\ne0}(x-a)$, which factors as $(x^5-1)(x^5+1)$ above.

**Up to isomorphism.** As before: monic polynomials are the same class when one becomes the other under $x\mapsto\lambda x+\mu$ ($\lambda\ne0$); there are $10\cdot11=110$ substitutions. Since $11\nmid d$ for $d=2,3,4$, each class has a **depressed form**, and the substitution $x\mapsto\lambda x$ acts on the depressed coefficients $a_i\mapsto a_i\lambda^{i-d}$.

## Functions on GF(11) are polynomials

Every function from $\mathrm{GF}(11)$ to itself is a unique polynomial of degree at most $10$ ($11^{11}$ of each), because $x^{11}=x$.

**Exponentials need a lift.** $a^x$ has $x\in\mathbb Z/11$ but the exponent lives in $\mathbb Z/10$. With the balanced lift $x\in\{-5,\dots,5\}$ (the vault's convention), the values at $\bar5,\dots,5$ and the interpolating polynomial:

- **Reflection:** $a^{-1}$ gives the polynomial of $a$ at $-x$. The pairs are $2\leftrightarrow\bar5$, $\bar4\leftrightarrow\bar3$, $3\leftrightarrow4$, $5\leftrightarrow\bar2$; the polynomial of $\bar1=\bar1^{-1}$ is even.
- **Factor shapes.** Each nontrivial exponential has an irreducible **quadratic** factor. The four primitive bases have shape $2+8$ (a quadratic times an irreducible octic); the order-5 bases have shapes $2+4+4$ ($3$ and $4$) and $2+2+6$ ($5$ and $\bar2$); and $(-1)^x$ has $2+4+4$ with the two quartics swapped by $x\mapsto-x$.
- **Not like $\mathrm{GF}(5)$.** There the exponentials split into primitive quadratics. Here there is no such clean pattern.

The exception is $\log_{\bar4}$, which has a **triple** root at $1$ and the shape $x\,(x-1)^3\cdot\text{quadratic}\cdot\text{quartic}$. The bases come in inverse pairs ($2,\bar5$ and $\bar3,\bar4$), so $\log_{a^{-1}}=-\log_a$ as integers mod $10$; the lifts to $\mathrm{GF}(11)$ are not a ring map, so the polynomials are not negatives.

**The other bases are partial.** Elements of order $5$ ($3,4,5,\bar2$) generate only the subgroup of squares $\{1,3,4,5,\bar2\}$, and $\bar1$ generates $\{1,\bar1\}$. Their logarithms exist only on those subgroups, with values in $\mathbb Z/5$ or $\mathbb Z/2$. Setting everything outside the subgroup to $0$, as with $\log0$, gives polynomials. **That is a convention, and the answer depends on it.**
